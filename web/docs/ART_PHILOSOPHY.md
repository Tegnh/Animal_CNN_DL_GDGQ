# Sounding — a visual philosophy

**Sounding** is the old survey practice of lowering a line to learn how deep the water is.
The movement treats every surface as the top sheet of something deeper, and treats the act
of measuring that depth as the subject of the image.

**Space and form.** A composition is a stack of cut sheets, never a single plane. Forms are
closed, irregular loops nested inside one another, each one a slice taken a little further
down. No loop is drawn by hand; each is the consequence of a height that was measured, and
the eye should sense that the curves obey a rule even where it cannot name it. The stack
sits off-centre and is allowed to leave the frame, so the page feels like a window onto a
larger chart. Every curve is meticulously resolved: no kinks, no stray points, the product
of patient, expert refinement.

**Colour and material.** One hue only, taken through six depths, from sage paper at the
shore to a green so dark it reads as ink at the deepest sounding. Depth is tone, not shadow:
there are no gradients, no glows and no blur, only flat sheets with a hairline edge where one
sheet ends and the next begins. A single warm clay is held in reserve for the place where
the instrument fails to find the bottom. The calibration of the six tones is painstaking;
each step must be distinct in print and on a poor screen alike.

**Scale and rhythm.** One mass dominates and is very large. Around it the rhythm is that of
an instrument: evenly spaced ticks, a thin rule, a coordinate. The spacing between contour
loops does the work that shading does elsewhere: where loops crowd, the ground falls
steeply; where they open, it rests. Repetition is the craft here, and it should feel like
the labour of countless careful hours rather than an effect.

**Composition and hierarchy.** Text is a label on a specimen: one large word, then a few
small mono annotations set like chart marginalia, aligned to the same hairlines as the
drawing. Nothing overlaps, nothing touches the edge by accident, and every margin is
deliberate. Emptiness is the shallow water of the page and is given as much attention as the
dense parts.

**Craft.** The finished plate should look as if a cartographer at the top of the field spent
weeks on it: every line weight chosen, every tone tested beside its neighbours, every label
placed to the pixel. It is an artefact that claims something invisible can be surveyed with
enough care. The reference to layered machines is never drawn; it is only felt, in the fact
that the image is made of layers and gets deeper as you look.
